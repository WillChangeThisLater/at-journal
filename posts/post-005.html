Average Day in the Life of a Thru Hiker

Date: 2026-05-09 | Mile: 470 mi

Let me walk you through an average day in the life of a thru hiker.

6:30

You wake up to the silent alarm rattling in your sleeping bag. There's a soft rain pattering against your tent. Your feet and legs have a dull ache that you've come to expect early in the morning. You lie still for a few minutes, trying to convince yourself to sleep in. You have 20 miles to do today. Maybe you can still make it if you start at 9 instead of 7? But you can't and you know it. You summon your strength, prop yourself up, and uncap your sleeping pad. The air comes hissing out. You're committed now. The day has begun.

6:45

Time to pack up. You press the air out of your sleeping pad, then fold it up and place it in the trash compactor bag in the bottom of your pack. That trash compactor bag is your "dry bag", and is the last line of defense between your gear and the rain. You shove your sleeping bag and pillow into the bag. Ok. Time to leave the tent. You go outside, pull your pack out of the vestibule, and start taking down the tent. You unclip the inner fly and shove it into the tent bag. You then remove the trekking poles that are holding up the outer fly. The outer fly collapses to the ground. You zip everything up, unstake the corners, then shove the outer fly into the tent bag. Great. You shove your food bag into the pack right above the trash compactor bag. You've been told to do this to create a "solid base" for the pack: your food bag is your heaviest item, so you want the weight snug and right against your back. You put your stove, pot, and tent right above the food bag. You've done this so much it is almost mechanical. You make sure everything is snug, then clip the top of the pack to the bottom and pull the straps tight. You treat yourself to a half crumbled pop tart for breakfast. No time for anything fancy. You must be on your way.

7:00

You hit the trail. Your body feels stiff, but good. The rain has calmed down a bit. The trees and mountains are wet and shrouded in mist. It's quite beautiful. You normally hike in silence for the early hours of the day, alone with nature and your thoughts. Coming into the trail you thought you would be thinking very deep thoughts about the meaning of life, your future hopes and dreams, etc, etc. Those thoughts come sometimes, but more often than not you find yourself immersed in nature, listening to the birds singing in the trees.

9:00

You've been hiking for a couple hours now. You're a bit bored, and a bit hungry. You throw down your pack and pull out a jar of peanut butter which you immediately start digging into with your spork. Eating raw peanut butter is a bit gnarly, but it tastes ok and you need all the calories you can get. You've found that you need to continuously snack on the trail: the general rule of thumb is to eat something every 30 minutes. You employ a combination of protein bars, beef jerky, peanut butter, and candy bars to keep the calories coming. Even still, you know you're fighting an uphill battle. You've gotten to the point where you'll force down a Butterfinger bar not because you want to, but because you have to. It's hard to keep up.

Ok, time to hike again. You throw on your pack and do a mental check. Does the hip belt feel good? Yes. Are the load balancers decent? Yep. Is there too much weight on your shoulders? Ah, yep... you loosen the shoulder straps a bit and lower the tension. Great, ready to go.

No nature sounds this time though. Time to get to work. You throw on Spotify, set the volume to low so you can loosely hear what's going on, and head off.

11:30

You've been hiking for a couple more hours now, and your feet are beginning to hurt. You have a couple blisters from weeks ago, but they have healed up nicely and haven't been bothering you recently. More concerning is the pain you feel at the back of your ankles and in the tips of your toes. You've felt this pain for days. You're not sure if the pain is normal or abnormal. Hiking 12 hours a day with a weighted pack for weeks on end isn't exactly natural, and most of the thru hikers you've talked to have some kind of foot pain or other. Stretching and ibuprofen help, but 20 miles is bound to do a number on any pair of feet, no matter how worn and callous. You do a mental scan. Many things feel somewhat off, but does anything feel really off? No? Great. You trek on.

The sun is finally starting to peak out. You decide to take an early lunch. You find a sunny spot on the side of the road. You put your pack down, take out your tent bag, and lay your outer tent fly on the grass to let it dry. You sit down and pull out your food bag. Time to eat! You grab a Cliff protein bar, some summer sausage, and a block of cheese, and begin to chow down. You eat so quickly you barely taste the food.

You decide to make some tea to wash everything down. You take out your stove and pot. You add water to your pot, then attach the stove to the gas canister and slowly turn the fuel knob until you hear the slow, almost silent hiss of gas. You grab a lighter, flick it on, and watch as a tiny fire bursts to life at the base of the stove. You throw the pot on the stove. It'll take a few minutes for the water to boil. You're happy to wait.

As you wait, an Uber pulls to the side of the road. A hiker you've never seen before steps out. You get to talking. He introduces himself as "Stay Out". Turns out he is a fellow Jersey boy who is getting back on the trail after taking a week off for a family emergency. He says he is planning on putting in serious miles - 20 to 25 a day - to catch up to the folks he was hiking with before. You feel a twinge of pity. Making up a week of time isn't easy, and you know he has his work cut out for him. You wish him luck and watch him head onto the trail and over the hill.

Oh shit, the water is boiling over! You turn off the fuel and let things simmer down for a few seconds before taking the pot off the stove. You throw in your tea and sip away. It's tasty. You sit in silence for a while, basking in the sunlight. It feels good, but you know you can't sit too long. You enjoy the moment, then take a breath, get the pack up, and keep moving.

12:30

You've finally hit the big climb for the day. You stop for a moment at the base of the climb and look up briefly before thinking better of it. You chug a Propel-infused water and munch on a Cliff protein bar. Ok. Time to lock in. You start playing your hill climbing playlist and take a couple deep breaths. Now you're ready. You look down at the ground and begin to climb.

One thing no one told you about the AT is how much you'd be looking at the ground. The ground, it turns out, is something of an enemy. Roots splay over the trail hoping to trip you. Sharp rocks jut out at awkward angles waiting for you to misstep so they can twist your ankle. Snakes slither in the grass, not wanting to bite you but not hesitating to either if you get too close. You heard somewhere that an average thru hiker takes 5 million steps to get to Maine, and it dawned on you quite early that one misstep, just one, could cost you your entire hike. You don't want that to happen here, on this climb, still so far away from Katahdin.

1:00

This has been a long climb. Your legs are burning and your breathing is staggered. You keep thinking you're close to the end, but every time you think you've reached the top the trail just keeps going up. You curse softly, and nothing in particular, and trudge on.

Climbing is funny. Climbs are never easy, but a good climb can be one of the most rewarding parts of the trail. It comes as little surprise to you that the views at the end of climbs, especially tough climbs, can be spectacular. You've found you also like the way your body feels after a really tough climb. Nothing makes you feel more like a hiker than reaching a summit, heart pounding so hard you can feel the blood against your skull, hands so sweaty you almost lose your grip on your trekking poles. All this to say, summits can sometimes be quite rewarding. Sometimes.

1:30

You've reached the top. Turns out this summit was a PUD (Pointless Up and Down). No view at all. Boo. You take a second to catch your breath. You wolf down your third protein bar for the day. Time to trek on.

3:00

You've been hiking down, down, down for a while now. It's been a quiet few hours. You haven't seen many people. But that's about to change.

Trail angels are folks who spend their time, money, and energy to help out hikers at random points throughout the AT. You don't know when or where you will meet trail angels, but when you do they are always welcome company.

You hike down all the way to a road. There's a van a few feet in front of you with a couple in front of it. They beckon you over and introduce themselves as trail angels, "Lost" and "Found". Turns out they hiked the trail a few years ago and loved it so much they decided to give back whenever they can. They pop open a cooler with soda and PBR, and open a bag with little Debbies and clementines peaking out. You grab a soda and a little Debbie and start chowing down.

You're savoring the last sips of soda when you see another hiker far down the road. They are too far off to make out, but they are slowly coming towards you. Eventually you make out a face... Kiwi! Kiwi is one of those hikers with a perpetual upbeat attitude who everyone knows and likes. Today, though, he looks to be in great pain. He gets to the van, throws his pack down, and exclaims "18 fucking miles. I walked 18 miles on a swollen calf. I knew it was swollen when I woke up but just didn't want to believe it". His calf is, indeed, swollen to roughly the size of a bowling ball. There's silence for a second. No one says anything, but everyone knows - Kiwi is going to have to come off trail. Maybe for a few days, maybe for a week. Maybe even longer.

Suddenly everyone springs into action. You grab a cork ball out of your pack and roll it to him so he can massage his calf. The trail angels grab a plastic bag, fill it with ice from the cooler, and give it to him so he can bring the swelling down. You throw him a PBR, which he decides to shotgun. You can see his mood begin to lift. His friends, Radio and Jeopardy, come hustling to the van. Turns out they already called a shuttle that will take all of them to a nearby town for a fun night. The shuttle pulls up almost instantly and the weary hikers pile in. They are off before you know it.

You smile at no one in particular. You feel the best you've felt all day. There's something that feels incredibly good about helping others, even if all you did was lend them a massage ball and toss them a beer.

4:00

You float up the last hill of the day, fueled by trail magic and thoughts of dinner. You eventually hit the top. There are a bunch of campsites scattered across the top. One in particular has a beautiful view of the surrounding mountains. You grab it and set up camp. You pitch your tent, blow up your air mattress and pillow, and pull out your food bag for later.

5:00

Time for an early dinner. You set up your stove, attach it to the fuel canister, and light it. You make ramen with mashed potatoes, a classic. You eat slowly, taking everything in. While you eat you do a bit of planning for tomorrow: finding water sources, scouting potential tent sites, etc.

6:15

Dinner is done. You've cleaned up, packed everything away, and even brushed your teeth. Your last task for the day is simple... stretch. Most hikers stretch at the end of a long day, and you are no exception. You don't exactly know what you're doing, so you make it up as you go along. Most of your stretches center around your legs and feet.

7:00

Your day is done, but it's only 7. You decide to treat yourself before bedtime with a mint tea. You boil water, throw in the tea bag, and start sipping. The sun is setting slowly over the hills. You absorb the moment. It's been a good day.

7:30

Time for bed. You hop in the sleeping bag and turn to your side. At home it takes you hours to fall asleep. Out here, you are out like a rock in minutes. You know you need to be up by 7 tomorrow, to do everything all over again. You turn to your side. Your breathing gets heavy. Your thoughts fade out. The darkness arrives. Zzzzzz
